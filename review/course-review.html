Course · Design trials

REVIEW / 26 OCTOBER 2026

AI and Digital Innovation

The course has a strong foundation: 99 authored lesson decks, practical labs, ethical questions and a substantial project strand. The best next step is to make the learning more deliberate: fewer unsupported assertions, clear independent tasks, and evidence that pupils can apply what they have learned.

What changed

  • Design: an editorial palette, bounded headings, numbered pathways and quieter cards. Three working design directions are available. The main layout uses the existing Haileybury font and magenta.
  • Lesson player: lessons request fullscreen when opened. If browser policy prevents it, a full-window fallback opens instead. Reading view uses natural-sized text, a maximum reading width and scrolling. “Fit whole slide” remains available and never scales above 100%.
  • Layout fixes: notes stay closed until requested, the mobile navigation fits, and video containers preserve 16:9. Progress IDs and slide positions remain stable.
  • Content: environmental cost and regulation lessons rewritten, policy-audit introduction corrected, historical bias-study framing qualified, model/product distinctions added. The obsolete environmental diagram and old checkpoint questions for the rewritten lessons are removed from the rendered course.
  • Independent learning: all authored activity slides have a solo route; six lessons have bespoke solo experiments. Summary slides include a closed-book explanation check. Videos have a watch-and-think task. Three additional optional videos have text alternatives.
  • Voice: homepage copy now makes direct invitations to investigate and judge. Some sensational companion-course titles are replaced. This is an editorial pass, not a claim that every line of the legacy course has been rewritten.
  • Maintenance: cache versions updated, missing companion content added to offline installation, offline misses return a proper response, and the standalone Passport export is rebuilt.

Content judgement

The foundations are useful; the course does not need wholesale replacement. The highest risk is confidently presented, fast-changing detail. In particular, “fully active in 2025” misrepresented the EU AI Act’s staged application; per-query environmental figures were presented with unjustified precision; and the 2018 Gender Shades results were described too broadly. Those examples are corrected in the live lesson data.

The inventory below is a structural review of every deck, with automatic flags to guide further editorial review. It is not independent verification of every historical anecdote, statistic, legal claim, source or external link. The named-model comparisons, companion case studies, copyright lessons and remaining environmental material deserve a source-by-source pass next. Legacy Word, PowerPoint and PDF downloads remain historical versions and have not been rewritten.

Sources checked for the changes

  • European Commission: AI Act implementation timeline — distinguish entry into force from the application of particular obligations.
  • IEA: Key Questions on Energy and AI — system boundaries, infrastructure demand and uncertainty.
  • Buolamwini and Gebru: Gender Shades (2018) — commercial gender classification, not a universal present-day facial recognition score.
  • DfE: generative AI and data protection in schools — replace an unsupported blanket deadline with current guidance.

Videos worth using

  • 3Blue1Brown: Large Language Models explained briefly — ask why fluent output is not proof.
  • 3Blue1Brown: But what is a neural network? — ask what training changes. Treat the mathematics as optional extension for younger pupils.
  • Code.org: Training Data & Bias — ask why an overall score can conceal unequal outcomes.

Titles and publisher pages were checked. Actual playback on the school network, captions and continued embedding availability need a device check. The additions link out rather than loading a third-party player automatically; written alternatives keep the learning available.

What would make this exceptional?

  1. Make every lesson end in something worth assessing. A decision memo, annotated output, test log or small prototype. Judge explanation, evidence and revision, not clicks or time spent.
  2. Use a consistent learning sequence. A scenario and prediction; a short explanation and worked example; independent application; feedback; a new transfer problem. Keep the explanation close to the task.
  3. Build a progression of scenarios. Start with a wrong homework answer, move to conflicting sources and unfair decisions, then a project with a real stakeholder. Return to earlier cases as pupils learn more.
  4. Add worked responses at different standards. Show an adequate answer, a stronger answer and why it is stronger. Keep them behind a reveal after pupils attempt the task.
  5. Separate completion from mastery. Current certificates mainly represent completion. Introduce a small evidence portfolio, delayed retrieval and a short explanation or viva before claiming secure competence.
  6. Offer support and stretch. Shorter explanations, glossary support and a scaffolded starting point alongside optional technical depth. Test with pupils using assistive technology, tablets and slow connections.
  7. Give content an owner and review cycle. Each volatile claim should have a source, checked date and next review date. Run termly checks of links, product guidance and legal material; avoid maintaining disconnected copies of lessons.
  8. Evaluate learning, then publish what works. Pilot with a small mixed-attainment group. Compare an unaided task before, immediately after and several weeks later. Ask pupils where they became stuck and observe, rather than relying only on satisfaction scores.

Still needed from the recent teaching work

The recent lesson developments mentioned in the brief were not supplied or identified. This branch improves the repository’s existing course; it does not claim to incorporate those unseen materials. Reconcile them with the lesson IDs below before deciding what to replace or add.

Validation and limits

Node checks cover all JavaScript, 99 authored decks and 102 authored quiz answers. Browser rendering covered 59 AEP lessons, 40 Removes lessons and 24 reused Passport lessons. Responsive checks cover 1440, 768 and 390 pixel widths, next/back, closing, reading view, fit view and the denied-fullscreen fallback. Top-level HTML local links resolve. Native Chromium fullscreen, cached offline Passport lessons and the rebuilt standalone Passport also passed. Real school logins, teacher administration, every lab interaction, external video playback and Safari/iPad hardware have not been end-to-end tested.
